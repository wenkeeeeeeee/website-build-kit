# Colour: mood pair and accent

A palette move from current brand posts on Instagram, tried on finished sites. **It's optional, and the builder
decides.** Any variant, V4 included, may use it when the client's products, brand or design suit it well; otherwise the
palette comes from the references as usual. Never use it just because it's here;
the business, its brand colours, the tone and the references always win. It's a way of building a palette, not a
list to pick from: every palette is worked out fresh from the client's own logo, photos, products and place.

## The move
- **A mood pair:** two shades of one colour family set the mood, one deep and one light or mid (navy with stone blue,
  plum with blush, chocolate with cocoa cream, deep teal with mint). Find the family in the client's world.
- **One accent from the other side of the wheel,** strong and saturated: blue meets scarlet, pink meets electric blue,
  violet meets acid lime, raspberry meets butter yellow, green meets coral or pink, brown meets lime or cobalt. It should
  look slightly wrong for a second, then right.
- **Three colours do the work.** Neutrals for body text and photo grounds can sit beside them; nothing else competes
  with the accent.
- **A brand colour usually becomes the accent** (tuned brighter or deeper so it pops) and the mood pair is chosen to set
  it off. A pale or pastel logo goes soft on a mid tone: keep the mood pair around it, not under it.
- Variations are welcome: the accent can sit closer on the wheel, the pair can be two neutrals with a temperature, the
  accent can come from a real object (a jam, a tape, a stage light). Judge by eye beside the client's photos.

## Finding the palette
The best combination for a client is usually not one listed here. Explore every time:
1. **Start from the client:** the logo and brand colours, their photos and products, the place, and the trade's own
   materials and marks (timber and plaster, drawings and redline, a stage light, a fruit). The mood family comes from
   there.
2. **Look for fresh combinations:** search current brand-palette posts on Instagram, Pinterest, Behance and Dribbble
   for the category and the tone ("<category> brand colour palette", "<tone> palette accent"),
   and note pairings that clash well. Take the pairing, never a brand's look.
3. **Make 3-5 candidates** that differ in family and accent, not shades of one idea. Try each on the real hero material:
   a swatch strip and a quick frame of the hero with the accent in its planned places.
4. **Pick the one that fits** the business and tone best and still surprises. Say in your report why it fits and
   which candidates lost. If none fits, use the palette from your references.

## How loud, by tone
- **Playful:** the accent takes 20-30% of a screen: giant headline words, whole panels and tiles, stickers, hand-drawn
  arrows, underlines and circles, a script word over a photo, colour floods and every main action.
- **Modern and professional, luxury included:** about 5-10%: one oversized number or word, a hairline, a thin glow along
  the product's edge, one script word, the main action. Calm type, no stickers or doodles; marks only when they come
  from the brand's own world (a streetwear brand's ink splatter and pen marks).
- **Corporate:** 5% or less, and the accent carries information: today's date, a finished stage, the selected item, a
  measurement, a key figure, the main action. If you can't say what an accent mark means, remove it. Marks are ruled and
  precise, taken from the trade (dimension lines, a revision cloud, a progress bar, a route line). Light, clean grounds
  stay light; the project photos stay the heroes. The surprise is the less obvious working colour (coral rather than
  safety orange, cobalt rather than builder's yellow).
- The variant's own tone places it inside that range.
- Plan where the accent lands on the first screen. If it only arrives after the first scroll, the hero reads mild.

## Guardrails
- The logo and the brand's colours stay as they are.
- Contrast: a bright accent often fails small text. Keep a deeper shade of the accent for big words on light grounds and
  for buttons with small text, and a darker shade of the mood colour for text on the accent. Body text meets AA.
- Status colours keep their meaning: a red "open" dot reads as closed.
- Red with gold reads festive for Chinese clients and red with green reads as Christmas: avoid them unless the client is
  festive. Near-black with one acid-green or vermilion accent, and cream with terracotta, read as AI defaults unless
  they're the client's own colours.
- No variant has to use the move. Skip it when the business, tone or references don't suit it.

## What the trials showed
- A dark brand with one strong colour gained most: a new mood pair changed the whole feel, and its own colour, made
  louder, kept it recognisable.
- A site with a pastel logo stayed mild above the fold because the accent arrived after the first scroll, and the logo
  went soft on the mid tone.
- On a corporate renovation site with full-bleed project photos, the hero barely changed; the move showed in the plans,
  schedules, numbers and process, and the light, clean look survived. Judge corporate trials past the first screen.
